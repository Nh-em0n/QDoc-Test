# The Honey Trap Hitting the AI Chip War: Jensen Huang, Nvidia, and the Ancient Art of Weakening Powerful Men

**SANTA CLARA** — The scandal engulfing Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang is not merely a story of infidelity at the top of the world's most valuable company. It is a case study in how an intelligence tactic older than the Bible—the honey trap—has found its perfect modern target: a man who controls the hardware underpinning the global AI race and who reportedly has a direct line to the President of the United States.

Huang admitted to an extramarital affair with a woman identified by U.S. officials as Alice Huang (no relation), a former executive at Singapore-based Megaspeed, a company under investigation for allegedly diverting restricted Nvidia chips to China . U.S. intelligence has reportedly concluded that Alice Huang was recruited by China's Ministry of State Security to gather information on Huang and American AI chip technology . Beijing has denied the allegations, calling them "pure fabrication" and a "smear against China" .

But beyond the geopolitical shockwaves—Nvidia's market value reportedly plunged by over $430 billion across two trading days, and Huang has been called to testify before at least three congressional committees—the affair has revived uncomfortable questions about a tactic that has toppled powerful men for millennia .

### The Oldest Trick in the Book

The honey trap—using sexual attraction and intimacy to extract secrets or create leverage—is arguably the world's oldest form of espionage. Its archetype appears in the biblical story of Samson and Delilah, where a woman is enlisted by the Philistines to discover the source of Samson's strength, ultimately leading to his downfall .

The Cold War industrialized the practice. East Germany's Stasi deployed so-called "Romeo Spies"—trained young men sent to West Germany to romance lonely women working in government offices, sometimes for years, before asking them to pass secrets. The CIA's own historical accounts describe how these operatives studied their targets' vulnerabilities, engineered "chance" encounters, and built relationships that lasted decades . The KGB ran parallel operations, using women known as "swallows" to compromise Western diplomats and officials, often through blackmail enabled by compromising photographs .

The formula has remained remarkably consistent: identify a target with access, build trust through genuine-seeming affection, and extract what is needed—whether through pillow talk, blackmail, or simply the target's willingness to impress a lover.

### Why Powerful Men Fall

The Nvidia case illustrates why the tactic endures despite decades of counterintelligence warnings. Alice Huang was not a stranger with a fabricated identity. According to reports, she held a senior role at Megaspeed, a legitimate Nvidia customer that purchased billions of dollars in chips. She moved in the same business circles. Photographs from 2024 and 2025 show the two together at industry events in Taipei—arms around each other, a peck on the cheek .

This is what intelligence professionals call "social engineering"—the trap is built not on deception alone, but on access. "She came not as a random woman," said Aliia Roza, a self-described former Russian honeytrap operative, in an interview with Fox News. "She came to the business of her because... Megaspeed bought chips from Nvidia for $4.8 billion" .

The vulnerability of powerful men, Roza suggested, is precisely their confidence that they are in control. "You see the most powerful man become the most vulnerable he becomes," she said. "What was this pillow talk? What did he share to her?" 

### The Devil's Weakness

The moral dimension of the honey trap has long fascinated—and troubled—observers. The tactic exploits something more than poor judgment. It targets the human need for intimacy, for being seen and desired, and turns that need into a weapon.

Historically, the consequences have been devastating. French ambassador Maurice Dejean was recalled from Moscow in 1964 after an affair compromised him. British embassy employee John Vassall was blackmailed by the KGB with intimate photographs. Israeli nuclear whistleblower Mordechai Vanunu was lured to Rome by a female operative and abducted by Mossad . In each case, the trap succeeded not because the target was foolish, but because the trap was patient.

For Huang, the personal cost is already severe. He has apologized publicly, and Nvidia's board has launched an independent investigation. The geopolitical cost is still unfolding—and will likely be measured in congressional hearings, intelligence briefings, and the quiet calculations of adversaries who understand that even the most powerful men have weaknesses that can be reached through the heart.

As historian Phillip Knightley observed, no planning can rule out a deceitful romance turning real—or a real romance being turned into a weapon . The oldest trap in the book endures because it does not require a flaw in character. It requires only that the target be human.
